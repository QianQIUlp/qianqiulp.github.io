# I Do Not Owe a Resource One Use

When I saw Tibo announce that Codex users would receive a **double reset**, my first reaction was not happiness.

That was strange.

A platform gives away additional capacity; a user should be pleased. One immediate full reset and one reset stored for later sounded like extra room to work.

The thought that arrived instead was:

**Did I miss out?**

My natural seven-day reset was almost due. If the promotional reset arrived just before it, then the benefit merely pulled tomorrow's recovery into today. Someone who had used a manual reset three days earlier might skip several days of waiting.

My first conclusion was immediate and satisfying:

> They gained. I lost.

It gave the discomfort a clean story. The problem was that I thought I was calculating a quota. I was actually finding reasons for a feeling of deprivation.

## At first, I counted resets

One immediate reset plus one banked reset equals two. If each reset is a coin, whoever receives more usable coins wins.

But a reset is not a coin. It is a state transition.

If I am nearly at full capacity already, an immediate refill adds little. If someone else is several days away from recovery, the same refill changes much more. The distribution can be equal while the marginal value differs because the starting states differ.

This is not necessarily unfairness. It is geometry.

## Value is the movement between states

The useful comparison is not how many reset labels appeared on the account. It is what changed before and after:

> value ≈ new state − previous state

What waiting disappeared? What task became possible? Which interruption was avoided? Which window opened?

An immediate reset has low marginal value when natural recovery is close. A banked reset has low realised value when no future task needs it. The same banked reset becomes valuable if it carries a concentrated piece of work through a point where it would otherwise stop.

The uncomfortable translation error happens when I turn “this benefit moved my state less” into “something was taken from me.” Those are not the same statement.

## Equal on paper is not equal in experience

Platforms usually distribute benefits by rule, not by the exact marginal utility of every user. Two accounts can receive the same formal event and experience it differently.

I can acknowledge that difference without demanding that every difference become an injustice. Otherwise every benefit becomes a comparison machine. Instead of asking what it enables in my life, I ask whether someone else extracted more.

That question has no natural stopping point. There will always be someone whose timing, workload, or starting balance made the same benefit more useful.

## A banked reset is not inventory

The word “banked” tempts me to treat unused capacity as stored wealth. But capacity becomes value only when it meets an existing task.

A reset saved for later is an option. Options can be valuable, but they are not completed work. If no meaningful task arrives before the option expires, the realised value may be zero—and that does not retroactively turn the original gift into harm.

This distinction sounds obvious. Emotionally, I often ignore it. A visible balance feels like possession; expiration feels like loss; using it feels like recovery. The interface turns possibility into something that resembles property.

## The harder admission

Perhaps I did not lack resets. Perhaps I lacked work that deserved one.

That is less comfortable because it moves the problem from the platform to me. If I have a clear project, additional capacity is useful. If I do not, a reset cannot manufacture direction. It can only make aimlessness feel more urgent.

I wanted the resource to arrive with a task attached, as though having the fuel should answer where to drive.

It does not.

## Spending a resource is not progress

Resource systems make consumption easy to measure. Usage bars move. Credits decline. Entitlements are redeemed. None of those movements prove that the state of my work improved.

I can spend an evening “making use” of a quota and finish with no code I will maintain, no article I believe, no question answered, and no reusable understanding. The resource was consumed; the task did not move.

This is where “do not waste it” becomes dangerous. It promotes expenditure from a means into a goal.

I may not be maximising output at all. I may be maximising the sensation of not wasting.

## I was also defending my discomfort

My original fairness argument was not entirely false. Timing really does change marginal value. But a true observation can still be recruited by an emotion.

I kept refining the calculation because I wanted the calculation to legitimise the feeling: if I could prove that another user received more, then my disappointment would become objective.

That is a familiar move. I do not simply feel bad; I build a model in which feeling bad is the only rational response.

The model may contain valid pieces and still be serving the wrong purpose.

## How resource systems begin to schedule people

Once a resource becomes visible, scarce, and perishable, it starts issuing suggestions:

- The quota resets soon; use it now
- The credit expires this month; invent a workload
- The subscription was quiet this week; make up for it
- The benefit is available; do not be the person who left value behind

Nothing explicitly orders me to act. The system merely changes the emotional cost of inaction.

I begin with a tool intended to support a goal. Then the tool's accounting creates a new goal: justify the tool.

## This does not only happen with Codex

Cloud credit can persuade someone to deploy an architecture they do not need. A gym membership can turn rest into guilt. A streaming subscription can make entertainment feel like inventory. Reward points can make an unnecessary purchase resemble a saving.

In each case, the accounting layer whispers the same substitution:

```text
using the entitlement = receiving value
```

But the value depends on what changes in the underlying life. A workout that injures me is not redeemed value. A cloud service that adds maintenance to an unwanted project is not free. A film watched from obligation is not leisure recovered.

## What I should ask instead

Not “How much did I use?”

I should ask:

- What existing task did this resource support?
- What interruption or constraint did it remove?
- What durable thing remained afterward?
- What attention did it consume in exchange?
- Would I still choose the task if the resource were not expiring?

The last question is especially useful. If the answer is no, the resource may be creating the task rather than serving it.

## A reset can be fuel or a whip

With a real destination, extra capacity is fuel. It helps a refactor continue, carries a debugging session through the point of interruption, or gives a difficult essay enough room to become honest.

Without a destination, the same capacity becomes a whip. It keeps asking whether I am wasting something, and the fear of waste becomes the only source of motion.

The hidden cost of a benefit is not always money. Sometimes it is a new imaginary loss around which I begin reorganising attention.

## Before using the next benefit, I pause

I used to treat the existence of a resource as a reason to act. Quota available: use it. Credit expiring: use it. Subscription underused: catch up.

Now I want to interrupt that reflex:

> A resource must not create a task.
> It may only support a task that already exists.

Before I use it, I ask three questions.

Does it connect to a goal already in motion?

Will it leave code, writing, notes, experience, or some other thing I can keep using?

Will the attention it consumes cost more than the value shown on the account?

If all three answers are weak, I can let the resource expire.

That may look like waste in the ledger. Handing over my time, attention, and direction merely to prevent expiration can be the larger loss.

By the end of this essay, I care much less about whether I “fully captured” the double reset. What remains is a small pause I can use the next time a quota, credit, or subscription approaches expiry.

If there is work worth advancing, the resource is fuel.

If there is nothing to receive it, expiration is allowed.

I do not owe a resource one use.
